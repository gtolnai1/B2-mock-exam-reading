Anti-social: It’s adverts, not friends, which now dominate our social media

Social media platforms used to be about communication between friends – now many are increasingly becoming a type of short video entertainment centre. Their business goal is to increase the time people spend on their apps, which, as a result, increases the income these platforms make from advertising.

Aurelie makes herself a coffee, sits down in her beautiful garden not far from Paris and goes on her social media to relax. Her feed (main page) is full of videos of interior design, cats and other cute animals. ‘That's how it works: you click on bananas and they give you bananas,’ she explains. There are ads too – although they look just like the other posts – for beauty creams, a diet and towels. But no friends. You rarely see posts of your friends anymore.

While there remain private people who still post on some social media, the switch from communicating with people you know to looking through professionally made content from people you don't is even more significant among younger users.

Vanessa Lalo, a Paris-based clinical psychologist specialising in online behaviour, says "users have become more conscious that the traces you leave (on social media) stay there forever, and some no longer want to maintain relationships on social media that are not real friendships. Some don’t want to be exposed to the criticism that might be a risk when you post or to the feeling that their post will seem poor compared to all the professional content". However, Lalo adds, people haven’t stopped posting, rather they are posting different things and in different places.

"Teenagers now, for example, publish a lot of content, but it's mostly funny stories and new versions of existing material. The idea is to make people laugh, not to tell people about their lives."

That still happens, she says, but it’s moved from social media platforms to messaging sites. There's also been a move towards private chat groups within the more traditional social media. "These are much more private places where ads and content made by influencers do not appear," she says.

"What we're seeing is social media splitting in two," says social media consultant Matt Navarra, author of the Geekout Newsletter. "Big platforms are becoming more about entertainment and discovery. Private messaging sites are becoming the place people go to actually be social. The problem is, those kinds of spaces are harder for companies to earn money from."

Meanwhile, for those who miss what are fast becoming the old days when traditional social media enabled you to share a bit of your life, a joke or a point of view with people you more or less knew, there are tools within platforms, says Matt Navarra, that allow you to see mainly posts from friends and family. "People have a choice to go to a feed that gives them that," he says. But, in my experience, most people don't.

How elephants pass on crucial survival skills to next generations

Young elephants have a lot to learn - what to eat, what to avoid, how to behave around others. When they grow up around their mother and aunts, their development into well-behaved adults may seem natural and spontaneous. However, it was proven by the consequences of illegal hunting in the past that they do much better if they can follow the example of older animals.

Elephants in populations that lost or lack older individuals tend to have a lower chance of survival, spend less time with others and respond less accurately to threats, says conservationist Lucy Bates of the University of Portsmouth, who studies elephant behaviour. She led an analysis published last year analysing 95 scientific studies about elephant populations in Africa and Asia which had lost their older leaders.

When old elephants disappear from their communities, Bates and colleagues explain, so does their culture, which is transferred across generations. “They usually do survive when the elders are gone,” she says. “But many more detailed aspects of their behaviour may change.” This puts in danger not just the elephants themselves, but also the animals and people they live alongside.

One of the most famous examples of an elephant population that grew up without elders is the group of some savanna elephants who, after losing their parents, were moved into a park called Pilanesberg in South Africa in the 1980s and 90s. Their adult relatives had been shot in Kruger National Park - not by illegal hunters, but by park managers, who felt the number of elephants had become too high, and believed the youngsters’ abilities would be enough to survive.

These elephants were likely shocked, says conservationist Graeme Shannon of the Norwegian Institute for Nature Research, who studied the animals from 2007 to 2010. His experiments suggested they were short of crucial knowledge when compared to elephants from the large and healthy population in Amboseli National Park in Kenya, where he also worked.

To further understand this impact, Shannon and colleagues played sounds they had recorded from elephants in the park and elsewhere on large speakers in the back of a car. In Amboseli, groups led by older females clearly identified these sounds, looking to welcome a family friend, but gathering around the baby elephants in defence when they heard the sound of strangers approaching. In Pilanesberg, on the other hand, where elder females were missing, the elephants responded equally cautiously to all recordings, whether these were warnings or came from friends.

In Amboseli, young elephants gradually learn when to be careful by watching the adults in the group, says Shannon, and not to worry too much otherwise. “The extremely anxious behaviour of elephants who have lost their elder leaders must leave them exhausted,” he says.
